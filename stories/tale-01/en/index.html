Salma had worked at the old city stadium for eleven years, and she knew its sounds the way a mother knows her child's cry: the creak of the east gate when the wind rose, the click of the wooden seats as the night grew cold, the groan of the irrigation pipes beneath the grass. So when she first heard the murmur, she knew at once it was not one of the stadium's sounds.

It was two in the morning, and the stands had been empty since yesterday's match. She stood in the players' tunnel holding her flashlight, listening. A distant murmur, as if thousands of people were breathing together. Then it rose suddenly and took on a rhythm: a whistle, applause, and one long cry, like a goal.

She swung the flashlight toward the stands. No one. The seats sat in their silent rows, and empty chickpea-snack bags stirred in the breeze.

She told herself it was exhaustion. But the murmur came back the next night. On the third night she wrote the time in her notebook: two o'clock and seven minutes. And on the fourth: two o'clock and seven minutes. As if a match began every night at the exact same moment, with no one in attendance but her.

On the fifth night she took the recorder the broadcast crew used, set it on the edge of the east stand, and went home.

In the morning she sat in the maintenance room listening to the recording through headphones. The murmur, then the applause, then a commentator's voice she didn't recognize, speaking excitedly about a cup final between the City team and the Southern Club. She kept listening, feeling the cold creep into her fingers. Then the voice changed suddenly. It grew faster, and there was a tremor in it:

"Watch the upper section of the east stand... it's shaking... please, get back!"

Then screaming, then the recording cut off.

Salma stayed in her seat for long minutes, unable to move. The east stand, specifically. She knew its upper section was old, and that management had put off repairing it for three years because the budget "wouldn't allow it." And she knew the cup final would be held in nine days, and that every ticket had already been sold.

She went to the stadium manager. She didn't tell him "I heard the future" — she wasn't crazy. She said she'd noticed new cracks, and that a tremor could be felt whenever the crowd jumped. The manager gave the smile of a man worn down by requests and said, "Submit a report."

She submitted the report. Two days passed with no reply. On the seventh night before the final, the murmur came louder than ever before. This time she heard something new at the end of it: the groan of metal, heavy and slow, like a ship breaking apart.

In the morning she called an old friend from university, engineer Yusuf, and told him more plainly than she'd told anyone else: "I have no proof, but I'm asking you to look at the east stand's support beams. For my sake."

Yusuf came that afternoon under the pretext of an old visit, carrying a small bag, a hammer, and a flashlight. He climbed beneath the stand between the concrete pillars and struck the iron in three places. The first strike rang out clean. The second was muffled. The third... hollow, as if he were knocking on an empty can.

He came out from under the stand, his face pale. He said quietly, "The rust has eaten the iron from the inside. From the outside it looks sound. If this section filled with people and they jumped together..." He didn't finish the sentence.

Yusuf wasn't employed by the stadium, but he was a licensed engineer. That night he wrote an official report, signed it, and sent it to the municipality, with a copy to the football federation and a copy to the manager. He attached photographs of the hollow support beam.

The next morning Salma's phone rang. It was the manager. His voice was completely different now. "How did you know?" he asked. She said, "I've been listening to this stadium for eleven years." He didn't understand, and she didn't explain.

The upper section of the east stand was closed off, attendance was reduced, and after long debate the final was moved to another stadium. Some fans grumbled, and a local newspaper wrote about an "unjustified delay."

On the night the match was supposed to have been played, Salma stood in the middle of the empty stadium just before two o'clock. She didn't know if the murmur would come.

It came. But it was different. There was no whistling, no shouting. It was a quiet, long round of applause, like a hall bidding farewell to someone leaving the stage. It rose a little, then faded, and faded, until it became a whisper, and then there was nothing in the stadium but the sound of the wind and the creak of the east gate.

In the morning, when she listened to the recorder, she found a single sentence at the end of the recording, in the same commentator's voice, calm this time:

"The match has ended... and everyone is fine."

She never heard the murmur again. But to this day she still leaves her flashlight burning at the mouth of the tunnel, every night, in case someone else should come.
